# Methods of the density tests

Two exploratory tests that cross the satellite urban extent with census population, to see
whether a census tract's density helps define what the urban patch is. Neither produces project
figures: nothing here is in `CIFRAS_CANONICAS.json`, and every number in the reports is
generated by the scripts.

| Folder | Script | Test |
|---|---|---|
| `densidad_radios/` | `scripts/12_prueba_densidad.R` | Test 1: keep the urban extent only in dense tracts |
| `franja_densa/` | `scripts/13_prueba_franja.R` | Test 2: add the fringe only in dense tracts |
| `comparacion/` | `scripts/14_prueba_comparada.R` | Tests 1 and 2 side by side at the same cut |

Each folder has an index `REPORTE.md` comparing all agglomerations, with a table of links to
every agglomeration's maps. It also has one subfolder per agglomeration with its own
`REPORTE.md`, `img/` and `tablas/`.

## The problem both tests address

The satellite extent comes from GISD30 (impervious surface, 30 m) classified with the Angel /
Atlas of Urban Expansion 2016 method (step 04). "The city" is the set of connected patches
touching the analysis perimeter, made of four classes:

- **urban** and **suburban** built-up: pixels the satellite sees as built;
- **fringe open space**: unbuilt land within 100 m of urban or suburban built-up;
- **captured open space**: unbuilt gaps under 200 ha enclosed by the city.

Fringe and captured land are *unbuilt*, but Angel counts them as part of the city. This raises
two opposite doubts:

1. **The extent may be too large.** Built pixels and their fringe count as city whether or
   not anyone lives there: airports, industrial parks, warehouses, new lots not yet occupied.
2. **The extent may be too small, or the built-up part too small.** Older, coarser imagery
   (1990 above all) can miss buildings. Some of what Angel calls fringe in 1990 may be built
   land the satellite did not see.

The census can help with both, because it says where people actually live. The two tests use
it in different ways.

## Common base (step 11)

`scripts/11_radios_ciudad.R` runs once per agglomeration and feeds both tests.

- **Pairs of years.** GISD30 decade with the nearest census: 1990 with 1991, 2000 with 2001,
  2010 with 2010, 2020 with 2022 (the project rule).
- **Pixel codes**, inside a frame of the 2020 city plus 3 km, the same in all four years:
  - built-up of the city (urban, suburban);
  - open land of the city (fringe, captured);
  - built-up outside the city;
  - the rest.
- **The city** is recomputed exactly as in step 04, including the shared-patch rule. Where a
  patch touches two perimeters, as in Gran Buenos Aires and La Plata, each pixel goes to the
  nearer one.
- **Per census tract** (step 09 layers), it measures:
  - km² of city built-up and city open land inside the tract;
  - persons, households and dwellings.
- **Tract density on urbanized land:**

      dens_urb = persons of the tract / km² of city (built + fringe + captured) inside the tract

  This is not gross density (persons / whole tract area). A large peripheral tract that holds
  a dense neighbourhood and a lot of countryside is not diluted by the countryside.
- **Areas.** Each 30 m pixel goes whole to the tract that contains its centre. In Salta this
  matches exact pixel fractions to within 0.03 km² and leaves the same tracts on each side of
  every cut. `--exacto` uses exact fractions, about 40 times slower.

## Test 1 · keep the urban extent only in dense tracts

**Purpose.** Doubt 1: is the satellite extent counting as city land where few people live?
The test removes the parts of the extent that sit in sparsely populated tracts and checks what
that does to size, density and growth.

**Rule.** For a cut T, in persons per km²:

    patch(T) = the city inside tracts with dens_urb >= T

A tract below the cut leaves the patch **entirely, built-up land included**. Persons of the
patch are the persons of its tracts.

**Cuts.**
- 300, 500 and 1,000: the project's `U_DENS`, with 500 central (`scripts/rutas.py`).
- 2,000, 3,000, 3,250, 3,500, 3,750, 4,000, 4,500 and 5,000. The range between 3,000 and 5,000
  is sampled finely because in Salta that is where the patch changes shape.

**What to look at.**
- *Area kept vs persons kept.* A cut that drops much land and few people is removing extent
  where nobody lives: the satellite was overstating the lived-in city.
- *Growth by cut.* If the 1990-2020 growth rate falls a lot with the cut, much of the satellite
  growth is land with few residents.
- *Census only.* Whole tracts with gross density at or above the cut, no satellite. A
  reference for what the census alone would call the city.
- *Dense tracts the satellite barely sees.* Tracts dense by the census with under 10 % of
  their area in the satellite city: settlement GISD30 misses.

## Test 2 · add the fringe only in dense tracts

**Purpose.** Doubt 2: is some fringe actually built and unseen, mostly in the early years?
Here built-up land is never questioned. The census decides only whether a tract's fringe and
captured land count as city.

**Rule.**

    city(T) = built-up  +  fringe and captured land of tracts with dens_urb >= T

- Built-up always stays.
- The fringe is in the density's denominator on purpose. If a tract is dense *even counting
  its fringe as urban land*, that fringe behaves like city and is probably built.
- No cut gives the full Angel extent; an infinite cut gives built-up only. Every cut lies
  between the two.

**Cuts.** The same eleven as test 1, plus no cut and built-up only.

**Premise check.** If the older imagery misses buildings, the same people sit on less visible
roof, so *persons per km² of built-up land* should be higher in the early years. The report
gives it by year. It is a consistency check, not a proof: a city that was denser and then
spread out shows the same pattern.

**What to look at.**
- *% of the fringe added, by year.* A high share in 1990 means that year's fringe sits in
  dense tracts and is likely built. A low share in 2020 means the newer fringe is mostly empty
  land around scattered development.
- *Growth by cut.* Full extent vs density-filtered fringe vs built-up only.

## The difference in one table

| | Test 1 (`densidad_radios`) | Test 2 (`franja_densa`) |
|---|---|---|
| Question | Is the extent counting land where nobody lives? | Is the fringe really built land the satellite missed? |
| Built-up land | can leave, with its tract | always city |
| Fringe and captured | stay or leave with their tract | join only in dense tracts |
| No cut | Angel extent | Angel extent |
| Strictest cut | only the densest tracts' extent | built-up only |
| Tends to correct | an extent that is too large (mostly recent years) | a built-up that is too small (mostly early years) |
| Unit of decision | the census tract | the census tract |

Both use the same density, the same tracts and the same city. They differ only in what a dense
or sparse tract does to the patch. Read together, they say:
- in which years the satellite extent overstates the lived-in city (test 1);
- in which years the built-up understates it (test 2).

## Side by side at the same cut (`comparacion/`)

At a given cut T, both tests treat fringe and captured land the same way: they keep it in
tracts at or above T and drop it in the rest. **The only difference is the built-up land of
tracts below the cut**: test 1 drops it, test 2 keeps it.

    test 1(T) = built-up and fringe of the dense tracts
    test 2(T) = all built-up + fringe of the dense tracts
    test 2(T) - test 1(T) = built-up of the sparse tracts

`scripts/14_prueba_comparada.R` paints every pixel of the Angel city in one of four classes:

| Class | Test 1 | Test 2 |
|---|---|---|
| built-up in a dense tract | kept | kept |
| built-up in a sparse tract | dropped | kept |
| fringe or captured in a dense tract | kept | kept |
| fringe or captured in a sparse tract | dropped | dropped |

It draws one map per cut, with the four years as panels. By default the cuts are 500, 3,000,
3,500 and 4,000; change them with `--cortes-mapa`.

The orange class answers a concrete question: *how much of what the satellite sees as built
sits in tracts with few residents?* If it grows over time, recent built-up growth is landing
where few people live. That points to industrial land, warehouses, gated developments or lots
not yet occupied.

## Caveats common to both

- **The tract is the unit.** One dense block keeps or admits all the land of its tract. The
  census does not say where people live inside a tract.
- **Tract size changes.** Tracts are redrawn and multiply between censuses: 40,099 in 1991
  and 66,502 in 2022 nationally (step 09). The same cut is finer-grained in later years.
- **Tracts mostly outside the city.** A tract with under 10 % of its area in the satellite city
  can have a high density on its sliver of city, because its residents live mostly outside
  what GISD30 sees. The test 1 reports measure this, and outline those tracts in orange on the
  density maps.
- **Years are not simultaneous.** The 2020 satellite is paired with the 2022 census, two years
  apart.
- **The census source is not INDEC.** CEUR-CONICET tracts via Source Cooperative (step 09).
  Persons for 2022 are 0.9 % under the INDEC total.
- **Shared-patch rule.** Agglomerations sharing a patch get step 04's split, not their own
  free extents.

## Running

    Rscript Rpipe/scripts/11_radios_ciudad.R [--ciudades salta,jujuy] [--exacto]
    Rscript Rpipe/scripts/12_prueba_densidad.R [--ciudades ...] [--cortes ...] [--sin-mapas]
    Rscript Rpipe/scripts/13_prueba_franja.R [--ciudades ...] [--cortes ...] [--sin-mapas]
    Rscript Rpipe/scripts/14_prueba_comparada.R [--ciudades ...] [--cortes ...] [--cortes-mapa ...] [--sin-mapas]

- **Order.** Step 11 needs steps 04 (or 04b), 07 and 09. Steps 12, 13 and 14 need only 11.
- **`--sin-mapas`** skips drawing. Reports still show maps that already exist on disk, so it
  is the quick way to refresh tables and indexes.
- **Default set.** Without `--ciudades`, every agglomeration with a perimeter and a step 04
  output.
- **Time.** Step 11 takes about a minute per agglomeration (several for Gran Buenos Aires).
  Steps 12 and 13 take a few minutes per agglomeration, mostly drawing maps.
