    Methods

    Two exploratory tests that cross the satellite urban extent with census population, to see whether a census tract's density helps define what the urban patch is. None of the numbers here are project figures.

    The satellite extent comes from GISD30 (impervious surface at 30 m) classified with the Angel / Atlas of Urban Expansion 2016 method. "The city" is the set of connected patches touching the analysis perimeter, made of four classes:

    • Urban and suburban built-up: pixels the satellite sees as built.
    • Fringe open space: unbuilt land within 100 m of urban or suburban built-up.
    • Captured open space: unbuilt gaps under 200 ha enclosed by the city.

    Fringe and captured land are unbuilt, but Angel counts them as city. That raises two opposite doubts:

    1 · The extent may be too large

    Built pixels and their fringe count as city whether or not anyone lives there: airports, industrial parks, warehouses, lots not yet occupied.

    2 · The built-up may be too small

    Older, coarser imagery (1990 above all) can miss buildings. Some of what Angel calls fringe in 1990 may be built land the satellite did not see.

    The census says where people actually live. Both tests use the same measure for each census tract:

    density = persons of the tract / km² of the city (built + fringe + captured) inside the tract

    This is density on urbanized land, not on the whole tract, so a large peripheral tract with a dense neighbourhood and a lot of countryside is not diluted by the countryside. Satellite years are paired with the nearest census: 1990–1991, 2000–2001, 2010–2010, 2020–2022.

    Purpose. Is the satellite extent counting as city land where few people live? The test removes the parts of the extent that sit in sparse tracts and shows what that does to size and growth.

    patch(T) = the city inside tracts with density ≥ T

    A tract below the cut leaves entirely, built-up land included. With no cut, the patch is the Angel extent.

    What to look at.

    • Area kept vs persons kept. A cut that drops much land and few people is removing extent where nobody lives.
    • Growth by cut. If 1990–2020 growth falls a lot with the cut, much of the satellite growth is land with few residents.

    Purpose. Is some fringe actually built and unseen, mostly in the early years? Built-up land is never questioned; the census decides only whether a tract's fringe and captured land count as city.

    city(T) = all built-up + fringe and captured land of tracts with density ≥ T

    The fringe is in the density's denominator on purpose: if a tract is dense even counting its fringe as urban land, that fringe behaves like city and is probably built. No cut gives the Angel extent; "built only" gives the built-up alone.

    Premise check. If older imagery misses buildings, persons per km² of built land should be higher in the early years. That is consistent with the idea but does not prove it: a city that was denser and then spread out shows the same pattern.

    • % of the fringe added, by year. High in 1990 means that year's fringe sits in dense tracts and is likely built. Low in 2020 means the newer fringe is mostly empty land.

    At the same cut, both tests keep the same fringe and captured land: that of tracts at or above the cut. They differ only in the built-up land of tracts below the cut.

    test 2(T) − test 1(T) = built-up land of the tracts below T

    The light-blue class answers a concrete question: how much of what the satellite sees as built sits in tracts with few residents? If it grows over time, recent built-up growth is landing where few people live: industry, warehouses, gated developments or lots not yet occupied.

    Test 1

    Question: is the extent counting land where nobody lives?
    Strictest cut: only the densest tracts.
    Tends to correct an extent that is too large, mostly in recent years.

    Test 2

    Question: is the fringe built land the satellite missed?
    Strictest cut: built-up only.
    Tends to correct a built-up that is too small, mostly in early years.

    • Urban extent: GISD30 (Zhang et al. 2022), 30 m, classified with the Angel / Atlas 2016 method; shared patches between agglomerations are split by distance to each perimeter.
    • Census tracts: 1991, 2001, 2010 and 2022 censuses, CEUR-CONICET via Source Cooperative. Persons for 2022 are 0.9 % under the INDEC total.
    • Areas: each 30 m pixel goes whole to the tract that contains its centre. The figures here count only city land inside census tracts, so along coasts they can be slightly smaller than the full Angel extent.
    • Landsat: the composites used as the grid of the pipeline (step 02), true colour, stretched per image. Not every agglomeration has them; otherwise use present-day imagery.
    • The tract is the unit. One dense block keeps or admits all the land of its tract.
    • Tracts multiply between censuses, so the same cut is finer-grained in later years.
    • Years are not simultaneous: the 2020 satellite is paired with the 2022 census.

    Everything on this page is computed in the browser from the tract tables of step 11 (Rpipe/scripts/11_radios_ciudad.R), with the same rules as steps 12, 13 and 14. Data exported by 15_visor_datos.R.
